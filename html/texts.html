Annotated Bibliography

Updated on Oct. 9

DH & AI use

Liu, J., Wang, Z., Xie, J., & Pei, L. (2024). From ChatGPT, DALL-E 3 to Sora: How has generative AI changed digital humanities research and services? [Preprint]. arXiv.

Reviews applications of large language models in DH, focusing on the processing and preservation of ancient books, cross-cultural research, and cultural heritage. Discusses the challenges and opportunities these tools bring to DH research and services.

Ma, R., Dedema, M., & Cox, A. (2026). A dancing bear, a colleague, or a sharpened toolbox? The cautious adoption of generative artificial intelligence technologies in digital humanities research. Journal of the Association for Information Science and Technology, 77(6).

Explores how DH scholars are adopting and critically evaluating GenAI in their research through survey and interview. The findings reveal that there are divergent opinions within the DH community.

Pollin, C., Fischer, F., Sahle, P., Scholger, M., & Vogeler, G. (2025). When it was 2024 – Generative AI in the field of digital scholarly editions. Zeitschrift für digitale Geisteswissenschaften, 10.

Draws on experiments presented at a DHd 2024 workshop to map eight areas where LLMs are being applied in digital scholarly editing (e.g., transcription, normalization, named entity recognition, translation, summarization). Calls for standardized workflows and evaluation protocols. Flags concerns about bias, limited reproducibility, and dependence on commercial models.

Underwood, T. (2025). The impact of language models on the humanities and vice versa. Nature Computational Science, 5, 695–697.

Notes that although many humanists are wary of language models, researchers trained in the humanities are actively working with AI, not only using these models as tools but also seeking to shape them toward a more flexible, context-sensitive understanding of written culture.

AI use disclosure

Agha, R. A., Mathew, G., & Rashid, R. (2025). Transparency in the reporting of Artificial INtelligence–the TITAN guideline. Premier Journal of Science, 10, Article 100082.

Guideline to transparently reporting the use of AI in any manuscript in general.

Bao, A., & Zeng, Y. (2026). AI disclosure, moral shame, and the punishment of honesty. Accountability in Research, 33(3), Article 2542197.

Redefines the shame emotion that authors experience in the process of responsible disclosure of AI use. Discusses its causes from the perspective of moral emotions.

Chemaya, N., & Martin, D. (2024). Perceptions and detection of AI use in manuscript preparation for academic journals. PLOS ONE, 19(7), Article e0304807.

Survey 271 academics on whether ChatGPT use in manuscript preparation should be reported and run GPT-modified abstracts through an AI detector. Finds no agreement on what uses require disclosure (views differed by ethical perceptions, academic role, and English-language background). Adds that detectors often flag even grammar-only edits, making nuanced disclosure rules hard to enforce.

Do, H. J. G., Feldman, M. Q., He, J., Hwang, A. H.-C., & Kim, S. (2025). Navigating generative AI disclosure, ownership, and accountability in co-creative domains [Workshop]. CHIWORK 2025.

Cheong, I., Guo, A., Lee, M., Liao, Z., Kadoma, K., Go, D., Chang, J.C., Henderson, P., Naaman, M. & Zhang, A. X. (2025). Penalizing transparency? How AI disclosure and author demographics shape human and AI judgments about writing. [Preprint]. arXiv

Investigates how AI disclosure statements affect perceptions of writing quality through controlled experiment. Finds that both human and LLM raters consistently penalize disclosed AI use.

Fang, J., & Lee, M. (2025). What shapes writers' decisions to disclose AI use? [Preprint]. arXiv.  

Identifies and curates a list of factors that could affect writers' decisions regarding disclosure for human-AI co-created content.

Earp, B. D., Schüklenk, U., Savulescu, J., & Porsdam Mann, S. (2026). Against mandatory prompt disclosure in AI-assisted scholarship. AI & Society, 1-15.

Argues against requiring authors to submit all of their LLM prompts and outputs.

El Ali, A., Venkatraj, K. P., Morosoli, S., Naudts, L., Helberger, N., & Cesar, P. (2024). Transparent AI disclosure obligations: Who, what, when, where, why, how. In Extended Abstracts of the CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems (Article 342, pp. 1-11). Association for Computing Machinery.

Two workshops about European AI Act Article 52’s AI transparency obligations; proposes a set of 149 questions clustered into five themes and 18 sub-themes (HCI).

Glynn, A. (2027). Academ-AI: Documenting the undisclosed use of generative artificial intelligence in academic publishing. In K. Arai & P. Lorenz (Eds.), Intelligent systems and applications: IntelliSys 2026 (Lecture Notes in Networks and Systems, Vol. 2077, pp. 328–348). Springer.

Analyzes 768 published cases of suspected undisclosed AI use, identified through characteristic chatbot phrasing; these appear even in journals with high citation metrics and article processing charges; few are corrected after publication. Urges publishers to enforce their disclosure policies.

Hosseini, M., Resnik, D. B., & Holmes, K. (2023). The ethics of disclosing the use of artificial intelligence tools in writing scholarly manuscripts. Research Ethics, 19(4), 449-465.

Discusses ethical issues related to using and disclosing artificial intelligence (AI) tools when writing or editing scholarly manuscripts. Provides recommendations to disclose AI use in methods or introductions, including details like prompts and versions used.

Hosseini, M., Gordijn, B., Kaebnick, G. E., & Holmes, K. (2025). Disclosing generative AI use for writing assistance should be voluntary. Research Ethics, 21(4), 728-735.

Argues that it should be up to the authors of manuscripts to disclose their use of GenAI for writing assistance.

Kaebnick, G. E., Magnus, D. C., Kao, A., Hosseini, M., Resnik, D., Dubljević, V., Rentmeester, C., Gordijn, B., & Cherry, M. J. (2023). Editors’ statement on the responsible use of generative AI technologies in scholarly journal publishing. Medicine, Health Care and Philosophy, 26(4), 499-503.

Editors of several bioethics and humanities journals propose preliminary recommendations for authors, reviewers, and editors, including that generative AI cannot be listed as an author and that authors should transparently disclose its use to editors, reviewers, and readers. Note: Statement was co-published in several journals (e.g., Hastings Center Report, Bioethics).

Kobak, D., González-Márquez, R., Horvát, E.-Á., & Lause, J. (2025). Delving into LLM-assisted writing in biomedical publications through excess vocabulary. Science Advances, 11(27), Article eadt3813.

Tracks the excess use of LLM-associated style words across more than 15 million PubMed abstracts. Estimates that at least 13.5% of 2024 abstracts were processed with LLMs, reaching 40% in some subcorpora. Estimate offers a useful baseline for comparing actual AI use with disclosure rates.

Ma, R., & Burnette, T. (2026). AI disclosure as "clay": Ambiguous signals and editorial governance in scholarly communication. [PrePrint]. SSRN.

Finds that editors read AI disclosure as an ambiguous but consequential signal of authorial agency: it can make hidden practices visible, yet it can also trigger unjustified suspicion of authors. Identifies an interpretability gap in disclosure-based AI governance. Calls for a standardized framework with responsibility shared across all stakeholders in publishing.

Raman, R. (2025). Transparency in research: An analysis of ChatGPT usage acknowledgment by authors across disciplines and geographies. Accountability in Research, 32(3), 277-298.

Examines the regional and disciplinary distribution of acknowledging AI in academic publications.

Resnik, D. B., & Hosseini, M. (2025). The ethics of using artificial intelligence in scientific research: New guidance needed for a new tool. AI and Ethics, 5(2), 1499-1521.

Examines the ethical issues raised when using AI in research and offer recommendations for responsible use.

Resnik, D. B., & Hosseini, M. (2026). Disclosing artificial intelligence use in scientific research and publication: When should disclosure be mandatory, optional, or unnecessary? Accountability in Research, 33(2).

Reviews disclosure policies from publishers and influential organizations. Develops a framework for disclosure, arguing that disclosure should be mandatory only when AI use is intentional and substantial.

Tang, A., Li, K. K., Kwok, K. O., Cao, L., Luong, S., & Tam, W. (2024). The importance of transparency: Declaring the use of generative artificial intelligence (AI) in academic writing. Journal of Nursing Scholarship, 56(2), 314-318.

Reviews nursing academic research journals to explore the current state of transparency regarding generative AI use.

Yoon, A., & Oristaglio, S. (2026). When AI use becomes the norm: Researcher perspectives on AI disclosure policy and practice. Accountability in Research.

Interviews 14 researchers in bioinformatics and computational social science. Finds that the gap between AI disclosure policy and practice is sustained by fragmented, unverifiable self-reporting, a transparency paradox in which honest disclosure invites penalization, and disciplinary differences.

AI Use/Disclosure Policy Analysis

Alduais, A., Qadhi, S., Chaaban, Y., & Khraisheh, M. (2025). Utilizing generative AI responsibly and ethically for research purposes in higher education: A policy analysis. Serials Review, 51(3-4), 120-170.

Systematically analyzes 74 policy documents from around the world, including diverse institutional and regulatory perspectives. Finds that across all document types, transparency and disclosure are consistently emphasized as foundational to ethical research conduct.

Ganjavi, C., Eppler, M. B., Pekcan, A., Biedermann, B., Abreu, A., Collins, G. S., Gill, I. S., & Cacciamani, G. E. (2024). Publishers’ and journals’ instructions to authors on use of generative artificial intelligence in academic and scientific publishing: Bibliometric analysis. BMJ, 384, Article e077192.

Analyzes generative AI guidance from the top 100 academic publishers and top 100 scientific journals. Finds that guidance is missing at some top publishers and journals and varies widely where it exists and that among those that provide guidelines, the allowable uses of GAI and how it should be disclosed varies substantially.

He, Y., & Bu, Y. (2026). Academic journals’ AI policies fail to curb the surge in AI-assisted academic writing. Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences[, 123(9)

Analyzes 5,114 journals and more than 5.2 million papers. Finds that about 70% of journals have AI policies, mostly requiring disclosure, yet AI-assisted writing has risen sharply whether or not a journal has a policy.

Kim, E. (2024). Analyzing AI use policy in LIS: Association with journal metrics and publisher volume[. Scientometrics, 129(12), 7623–7644.

Examines AI policies in 232 LIS journals. Finds that most have no explicit AI policy. Adds that journals with higher citation rates, top-quartile journals, and journals from larger publishers are more likely to have one, which suggests that large publishers play a key role in setting AI norms in LIS.

Lund, B. D., & Naheem, K. (2024). Can ChatGPT be an author? A study of artificial intelligence authorship policies in top academic journals. Learned Publishing, 37(1), 13-21.

Examines the AI authorship policies of 300 top academic journals (ranked by SJR). Finds that nearly all policies that address the question prohibit listing AI tools as authors. Analyzes how policies treat the acknowledgment of AI use.
